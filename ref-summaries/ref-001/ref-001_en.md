## Background

The contribution combines a lecture text with exhibition panels. A 2020 exhibition in Mainz (organised by the state heritage authority) was accompanied by a complementary exhibition at the Hardenburg ruin, prepared by the Heimatverein Hardenburg. The main source is Ingo Toussaint's 1982 dissertation on the Counts of Leiningen. Seven panels were designed by the Koblenz heritage office.

## Key points of the lecture "The Counts of Leiningen as Pillars of Power (1128–1317)"

- **Framing:** Noble families supported emperors and kings. The Leiningen counts served the Staufer, Welf and other royal houses in a period of rival kings and papal conflict. The lecture covers ten ruling counts and three other prominent ones, alongside 19 kings and emperors.
- **Origins:** The name "Leiningen" first appears in 780 as a place name in a Lorsch donation. The family is linked to the Emichonen, who split after 1125 into the Raugrafen, Nahegrafen, Veldenz and Leiningen lines. Limburg Abbey near Dürkheim and Speyer Cathedral are tied to the Salian kings buried there.
- **Early counts:**
  - Emich II (first called "of Leiningen" in 1128) served Lothar of Saxony and probably founded Altleiningen and Höningen.
  - Emich III served Conrad III and Frederick I Barbarossa, including on the Italian campaigns.
  - Frederick I (Emich), 1187–1214, was active around Richard the Lionheart and the Third Crusade, switched from Otto IV to Philip of Swabia and back, and received the Limburg protectorate in 1205. He died in 1214 without male heirs.
- **Second house (Saarbrücken line):**
  - Frederick II (1214–1237) inherited through his Saarbrücken mother. From about 1206 he built the **Hardenburg** (spelled "Hartenburg" in the text) on Limburg abbey land and had six sons.
  - Dagsburg succession war (1225–1241): Simon's marriage to Gertrude of Dagsburg led to conflict; the Leiningens kept Dagsburg until 1789.
  - Heinrich of Leiningen became Bishop of Speyer, Berthold Bishop of Bamberg.
- **Division of 1237:** Frederick III continued the main line. Emich IV founded the Leiningen-Landeck line, backed the anti-kings and founded Landau (town charter 1274).
- **Frederick IV (1254–1316):** Served six kings, received imperial fiefs under Rudolf of Habsburg, took part in the 1298 battle of Göllheim on Albert's side; his son Frederick V fought for Adolf.
- **Final division 1317/1318:** Frederick V took the main line Leiningen-Dagsburg (seat: Altleiningen). Jofried took the line **Leiningen-Hardenburg** (seat: Hardenburg). Jofried served Henry VII on the Roman expedition and was present at the 1312 coronation.
- **Conclusion:** Around 1317 the family's holdings peaked, with lands in the Wormsgau, Speyergau, Alsace and Lorraine. The rise of the Palatinate later challenged them, but they remained the second-strongest power in the southwest until 1789.

## The seven panels

1. Family trees and coats of arms (after Toussaint)
2. Royal proximity: list of kings and the counts' presence at courts, journeys and campaigns
3. Scattered holdings of the Leiningen counties in the Palatinate (1237–1467)
4. Codex Manesse: image and song of a "Friedrich von Leiningen", with translation (the song itself is not reproduced here)
5. Crusade participation: Third Crusade (Frederick I, probably), Sixth Crusade (Frederick III, probably), doubts about Frederick II's part in the Fifth; also Jofried's Roman expedition and the Brescia illustration
6. Reconstruction of the Limburg Abbey complex
7. Partnership between Hardenburg and Dabo (Dagsburg) in France, castle history and planning for the 800-year anniversary in 2023

## Source-critical notes

The essay contains internal inconsistencies, such as differing death dates for Frederick III and varying statements about when construction of the Hardenburg began. These should be checked against Toussaint's dissertation.
